Big Tech, one layer deeper

Where Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Apple actually make their money, what each reported last month, and the question hanging over all of them: is record AI spending going to pay off?

AMoney engines BThe five companies CThe AI spending question DRegulators
If you only read one part

The short version

  • Each company has one profit engine paying for its AI bets. Office and Azure, Google Search, AWS, Instagram and Facebook ads, and the iPhone.
  • The clouds are booming. Google Cloud more than doubled, Azure grew 48% and AWS 39%. Each now has a backlog of signed future business above $500 billion.
  • Spending now outruns cash. Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Microsoft spent about $209B on capex in one quarter. Only Microsoft had cash left over. The others are borrowing and selling new shares to keep building.
  • Apple is the exception. It spends far less on AI data centers and sells hardware instead. Its risk this quarter is memory costs squeezing margins.

Part C

The AI spending question

For years these companies made so much cash they could fund anything from what they earned. In 2026 that changed. Capex grew faster than cash flow, so three of the four big spenders turned to borrowing (bonds) and selling new shares. New shares mean each existing share owns a slightly smaller slice, which is called dilution.

The servers they're buying also wear out in a few years. That cost, depreciation, will grow and weigh on reported profits for years, even if AI revenue keeps rising.

The case it pays off

  • Cloud growth is accelerating, not slowing
  • Backlogs above $500B each mean the demand is already signed
  • Microsoft shows AI can be funded from cash flow
  • Ad businesses are growing faster thanks to AI targeting

The case for worry

  • Free cash flow is negative at Alphabet, Meta and Amazon
  • Borrowing and new shares dilute owners and add debt
  • Depreciation will rise for years
  • Some big cloud customers are AI labs these companies also invest in, so the money partly goes in a circle

Reference

Words in this sector

Hyperscaler
A company running cloud data centers at giant scale: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and often Meta.
Backlog / RPO
Remaining performance obligations: revenue customers have signed for but that hasn't been delivered yet.
Free cash flow
Cash from operations minus capex. Negative means the company needs outside money to keep spending.
Dilution
When a company issues new shares, each existing share owns a smaller slice.
Depreciation
Spreading the cost of servers and buildings over their useful life. Rising capex now means rising depreciation later.
Finance lease
Renting a data center long-term in a way accountants treat like owning it. Some capex totals include these, some don't.
At-the-market offering
Selling new shares gradually into the market instead of all at once.
Operating margin
Operating profit as a share of revenue. Microsoft's is about 50%; Amazon's about 14%.
Check yourself

Seven-question quiz

Score: 0 of 7 answered correctly

1. Which company gets about 98% of its revenue from ads?

Meta is almost entirely an ad business. Alphabet relies on ads too, but Cloud and subscriptions now make up over a third of its revenue.

2. AWS is 18% of Amazon's sales but about 63% of its operating profit. Why?

Selling computing capacity is far more profitable per dollar than selling goods with shipping and warehouse costs.

3. What is a company's "backlog" or RPO?

Backlog shows future revenue already under contract. Microsoft's is $677B; Google Cloud's is about $560B.

4. Alphabet made $40.6B of operating profit but had −$9.1B of free cash flow. How?

Free cash flow subtracts capex. Spending $51.4B against $42.3B of operating cash flow leaves a gap.

5. Why might existing shareholders dislike Meta selling $25B of new stock?

That's dilution. The money may be well spent, but each share's claim on future profits shrinks.

6. Why does Apple expect a lower gross margin this holiday quarter?

The AI-driven memory shortage raises the cost of every phone and laptop. It's the same squeeze hitting Nintendo and Sony.

7. Why does it matter that Microsoft's cash flow covered its capex while the others didn't?

Self-funded spending avoids dilution and new debt. Microsoft isn't the biggest cloud or the smallest spender; it just earns enough to pay for it.
