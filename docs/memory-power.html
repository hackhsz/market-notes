Memory, data centers and power, one layer deeper

What an AI chip needs besides the chip. Why memory makers are earning record profits while their stocks fall, how a company like IREN turns electricity into GPU rentals, and why power became the bottleneck and then a political fight.

AMemory BData centers CPower →What to watch
If you only read one part

The short version

  • Memory is in the biggest boom in its history. SK Hynix kept 80 cents of operating profit from every dollar of sales last quarter. Micron's quarterly revenue is nearly five times what it was a year ago.
  • But memory has always been boom-and-bust. New factories land in 2027–29, and investors are already looking ahead to that, which is why the stocks fell 16–21% this month despite record profits.
  • Neoclouds like IREN turn power and buildings into GPU rentals. They grow very fast, and they need enormous amounts of borrowed money to do it.
  • Power is now the slowest piece. Gas turbines are sold into the early 2030s, and the biggest US grid auction keeps clearing at its price cap. After this month's elections, voters angry about electric bills put data-center skeptics in charge in Texas and other key states.
Part A

Memory

Working memoryDRAM

Holds what a computer is using right now and forgets everything when the power goes off. In every phone, PC and server.

AI memoryHBM

DRAM stacked 8 to 16 layers high, wired through tiny vertical holes, and placed right beside a GPU. Hard to make, very expensive.

StorageNAND flash

Keeps data with the power off. Used in SSDs and phone storage. Also booming, but less central to AI.

Why HBM makes everything else scarce. The same factories make HBM and ordinary DRAM. HBM uses roughly three times as much silicon per unit of memory, so every wafer shifted to HBM removes much more ordinary memory from the market. TrendForce estimates HBM will take about 22% of DRAM wafers by the end of 2026 and about 30% by the end of 2027.

The result: ordinary DRAM contract prices rose about 90–95% in a single quarter in early 2026, then kept climbing. Phone, PC and server makers pay more, and memory makers' profits explode.

TrendForce · Luminix DRAM cycle report

The memory cycle, and where we are on it

Memory chips are close to a commodity: one maker's DRAM can replace another's. So the industry swings through the same loop again and again.

  1. Shortage

    Demand outruns supply.

  2. Prices soar

    Profits explode. Memory stocks rally.

  3. Build

    Makers order new factories and equipment, which take two to three years.

    We are here
  4. Flood

    New capacity from every maker arrives around the same time.

  5. Glut

    Prices crash and profits turn to losses.

  6. Cut

    Makers slash spending, supply shrinks, and the loop starts again.

Stocks usually turn before profits do. Investors sell around step 3, while results still look great, because they are pricing in steps 4 and 5. Bulls argue AI changes this cycle: HBM is sold on multi-year contracts, and SK Hynix says it's sold out through 2029.

Part B

Data centers and neoclouds

A neocloud rents GPU computing to AI labs and Big Tech, separate from Amazon, Microsoft and Google's own clouds. Several started as bitcoin miners, because mining had already forced them to secure cheap power and big buildings, the two hardest things to get now. IREN, formerly Iris Energy, is one of them.

How a neocloud makes money

  1. Lock down land and a grid connection. This is now the slowest and most valuable step.

  2. Sign a multi-year contract with a big customer, ideally with money paid up front.

  3. Borrow against that contract to buy GPUs, usually billions of dollars.

  4. Build or fit out the data center with power, cooling and networking.

  5. Collect rent on the GPUs every month.

  6. Pay back the debt before the GPUs go stale. New chip generations arrive every year, so old GPUs lose value fast. This is the whole business's central risk.

Worked example: IREN's Microsoft contract

Simple arithmetic on publicly reported figures, to show the shape of the business. Not company projections.

Contract value over 5 years$9.7B
Revenue per year ($9.7B ÷ 5)≈ $1.94B
Paid up front by Microsoft (20%)≈ $1.9B
GPUs and equipment bought from Dell≈ $5.8B
Years of rent to cover the hardware ($5.8B ÷ $1.94B)≈ 3 years

That's before paying for electricity, the buildings, staff and interest. It leaves roughly two years of the contract to earn a profit, and the GPUs need to stay useful for all five.

Part C

Power

How much power? One modern AI rack uses about as much electricity as 100 homes. A 1-gigawatt AI campus uses about the full output of a large nuclear reactor. US data centers used about 4.4% of the country's electricity in 2023, and Lawrence Berkeley National Lab projects 6.7–12% by 2028.

Why it's a bottleneck. Chips can be built in months. Power plants, transmission lines and grid connections take years, and the equipment to build them is sold out.

What's happening now

  • The grid is short of power, even at capped prices. PJM's July 2026 auction fell 6,831 MW short of its reliability target, and only about 525 MW of new power plants signed up. Without the cap, prices would have been about $555. The grid's independent monitor attributes about 40% of the auction's cost to data centers.

    PA Environment Digest · Avanza Energy · PJM report

  • Gas turbines are sold into the 2030s. GE Vernova (reported Oct 28) took about $29.3B of orders, up 96%, and its backlog hit $197B. It sold turbine slots for 2032 delivery, and its CEO said turbine prices are up about 50% from 2025.

    Call transcript · Turbomachinery · Benzinga

  • Vistra had a record quarter (reported Nov 6). Adjusted EBITDA, a common profit measure for power companies, rose more than 50% to $2.38B, and it raised its 2026 outlook. It signed a 20-year deal to power a data center from its Odessa, Texas gas plant. Management also said Texas power prices have been trending lower as crypto miners convert to data centers.

    Vistra release (SEC) · Call highlights

  • The midterms turned power into politics. Democrats ran on electricity bills and won governorships in Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Nevada and Iowa. Texas's governor-elect campaigned on a statewide data center moratorium. Campaigns spent more than $380M on TV ads mentioning data centers. Expect pressure for tech companies to pay more of the grid's costs. Vistra fell 15.4% on the news, GE Vernova 11.5% and Constellation 9.2%.

Bull case

  • HBM is sold years ahead on contracts, unlike past memory booms
  • Power and grid connections can't be built quickly, so existing assets gain value
  • Turbine prices up 50% shows real pricing power
  • Big Tech plans to spend even more in 2027

Bear case

  • Every memory maker is adding capacity at once, the classic setup for a glut
  • Neoclouds carry huge debt against GPUs that age fast
  • Voters and new governors want data centers to pay more, or to slow down
  • One or two customers make up most of the revenue at several companies
  • High interest rates raise the cost of everything here, since it's all built on borrowed money
Reference

Words in this sector

Contract price
The price big buyers pay memory makers under supply agreements. The main gauge of the memory cycle.
Wafer input
How many silicon wafers a maker starts each month, and what share go to HBM versus ordinary DRAM.
Run-rate (ARR)
Current monthly revenue × 12. Shows the pace today, not a full year's results.
Depreciation
Spreading an asset's cost over its useful life. For neoclouds, how fast GPUs are assumed to wear out decides whether they look profitable.
EBITDA
Profit before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Popular with power companies and neoclouds; it leaves out real costs.
Capacity auction
A grid pays plants to promise they'll be available years ahead. Prices are quoted per megawatt per day.
Interconnection
Approval to plug into the grid. Waits can run for years, which makes sites that already have it valuable.
PJM / ERCOT
The two grids that matter most here: PJM covers the mid-Atlantic and Midwest, ERCOT covers most of Texas.
Check yourself

Seven-question quiz

Score: 0 of 7 answered correctly

1. Why did memory prices for ordinary phones and PCs jump in 2026?

HBM and ordinary DRAM come out of the same factories. Each wafer moved to HBM removes about three times as much ordinary memory from the market.

2. Memory makers just posted record profits, yet their stocks fell 16–21% in a month. What's the most likely reason?

Stocks look ahead. In a cyclical industry, investors often sell at peak profits because they expect the new capacity to push prices down later.

3. What does a neocloud like IREN mainly sell?

Neoclouds buy GPUs (mostly Nvidia's), house them in their own data centers with their own power, and rent the computing out under multi-year contracts.

4. IREN reports an 86% margin "before depreciation." Why does that phrase matter?

GPUs cost billions and lose value fast. A margin measured before depreciation looks much better than the true profit after that cost is counted.

5. Which kind of power company earns a return approved by regulators?

Regulated utilities own the local grid. Regulators set what they can charge and the return they earn on what they build. Power producers sell at market prices instead.

6. PJM's capacity auction "cleared at the cap." What does that mean?

Regulators set a ceiling. Hitting it three auctions in a row, while still falling short of the reliability target, means supply can't keep up.

7. Why did power stocks fall after the midterm elections?

New governors in Texas and other states campaigned on electric bills, and Texas's on a data center moratorium. That could slow data center growth or shift grid costs onto tech companies.
