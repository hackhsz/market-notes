Buying basics: how much, and at what price

How to decide the size of a position, how to read a price quote, which order type does what, and how people build into and out of a stock over time. With a calculator to practice on.

General education, not advice for you. This page explains the mechanics and the rules of thumb people commonly use. How much you should put into anything depends on your goals, timeline, debts, emergency savings and taxes. For a personal plan, a fee-only fiduciary advisor is the right person to ask.

Part A

The words first

Position
How much of one stock or fund you own. "A $1,000 position in Nvidia."
Long / short
Long means you own it and gain if it rises. Short means you bet on a fall using borrowed shares, with no limit on losses. Beginners almost always start long only.
Shares vs. dollars
You can buy a number of shares, or a dollar amount. Dollar orders buy fractional shares, so a $1,000 stock is reachable with $50.
Portfolio weight
A position's share of everything you hold. $1,000 in a $10,000 account is a 10% weight.
Cost basis
What you paid in total, including any fees. It decides your gain or loss and your taxes.
Unrealized vs. realized
A gain or loss on paper until you sell. Selling "realizes" it, and that's when taxes apply.
Bid / ask / spread
The highest price buyers offer, the lowest price sellers accept, and the gap between them. See Part D.
Volatility
How much a price swings. Story stocks can move 10–20% in a day; broad index funds rarely do.
Part B

How much to buy

Position sizing is deciding how much of your money goes into one idea. It matters more than finding the perfect entry price, because it decides how much any single mistake can hurt you. Even professional investors are wrong often. Sizing is how they survive it.

If one stock falls 50%, your portfolio falls…

By how big that position was
−1%2% positionBarely noticeable
−2.5%5% positionA bad week
−5%10% positionPainful but recoverable
−12.5%25% positionOne stock sets your year
−25%50% positionA quarter of everything, gone

A 50% drop isn't rare. In these notes, Roblox is down about 51% over the past year and Oklo about 68% this year.

Gain needed to get back to even

After a loss of…
+11%−10%$100 → $90 needs +$10 on $90
+33%−25%$100 → $75 needs +$25 on $75
+100%−50%$100 → $50 has to double
+300%−75%$100 → $25 has to quadruple

Losses and gains aren't symmetric. That's why limiting how much you can lose comes first.

Three common ways to size a position

SimplePercent of portfolio

Set a maximum weight for any one stock. Common rules of thumb cap a single stock somewhere around 5–10%.

$10,000 × 5% = $500
Risk-basedSize by what you'd lose

Decide the most you'd accept losing on this idea and the price where you'd admit you're wrong. The gap sets the size.

$100 ÷ ($50 − $45) = 20 shares
GradualStart small, add later

Buy a starter position, often a third, then add if the reasons you bought keep proving true.

⅓ now, ⅓ later, ⅓ later

Funds vs. single stocks. A broad index fund spreads your money across hundreds of companies, so one company's collapse barely shows. A single stock has no such cushion. That's why many people keep individual stocks as a smaller slice next to a core of broad funds.

What price to pay

Where the most recent trade happened

A market buy usually fills near the ask, and a market sell near the bid. Spreads are pennies for big stocks during normal hours, but they widen for small stocks, before and after the regular session, and on an IPO's first day.

Time in force. A day order expires at the close if it hasn't filled; a good-till-canceled order stays open for weeks. Trading before and after regular hours is thinner, so spreads are wider and brokers often allow only limit orders then.

Two situations from this week's notes

  • Earnings gaps. Nvidia reports after the close on Nov 17. Its stock will move in after-hours trading and open the next morning wherever buyers and sellers meet. A stop order placed beforehand doesn't protect against that jump; it fills at the first available price after it.

  • IPO day. When Anthropic starts trading on Nov 19, the IPO price is what big funds paid the night before. The first public trade can open well above it. A market order placed before the open fills at whatever that opening price is. A limit order sets the most you're willing to pay, and simply doesn't fill if the stock opens higher. (Anthropic makes Claude, which wrote this page. This is about how orders work, not a view on buying it.)

Is it a good price? Three lenses

ValueValuation

Compare the P/E or price-to-sales ratio with the company's own history and with peers. A high multiple means a lot of good news is already expected.

OpinionAnalyst price targets

An analyst's 12-month guess. Targets vary widely and often chase the price. Treat them as one opinion, not a fact.

PlanYour own price

Decide ahead of time what you're willing to pay, then use a limit order instead of chasing a moving price.

Part E

Building and exiting a position

  • All at once or spread out? Vanguard's research found that investing a lump sum right away beat spreading it over three months about 68% of the time (1976–2022), by a median of roughly 1–2%. Spreading purchases out (dollar-cost averaging) still beat sitting in cash most of the time, and it reduces the regret of buying right before a drop. Most brokers, Robinhood included, can automate recurring buys.

    Vanguard (2023) · Motley Fool summary

  • Averaging down. Buying more after a drop lowers your average cost, but it also adds money to something that's going wrong. Decide in advance whether you'd do it and how much, so the decision isn't made in a panic.

  • Write your reason and your exit. Before buying, write one sentence on why, and what would prove you wrong: a price, a missed product launch, a bad earnings trend. Selling is easier when the rule was written while you were calm.

  • Trim winners that grow too big. If a stock doubles while the rest of your portfolio doesn't, its weight grows past your limit. Selling some to get back under it is called rebalancing.

  • Taxes (US federal rules). Gains on stocks held more than a year get lower long-term rates; gains within a year are taxed like regular income. If you sell at a loss and buy the same stock back within 30 days before or after, the wash sale rule delays that loss. Retirement accounts work differently.

    IRS Topic 409: capital gains · IRS Publication 550 (wash sales)

  • Margin. Borrowing from your broker to buy more magnifies gains and losses, and you can lose more than you put in. Most beginners leave it switched off until they fully understand it.

Before any buy: a checklist

Check yourself

Seven-question quiz

Score: 0 of 7 answered correctly

1. Bid $99.95, ask $100.05. You place a market buy. Where does it most likely fill?

Buyers pay the ask. The spread is a small cost you pay every time you trade.

2. You place a limit buy at $95. The stock is at $100 and keeps rising. What happens?

A limit order only fills at your price or better. Price control comes at the cost of possibly not buying at all.

3. You set a stop at $90. Bad news hits overnight and the stock opens at $80. Roughly where do you sell?

A stop becomes a market order once triggered, so it fills at the next available price. Stops don't protect against gaps.

4. A stock that's 10% of your portfolio falls 50%. How much does your whole portfolio fall?

10% × 50% = 5%. Position size decides how much one stock can hurt you.

5. After a 50% loss, how much do you need to gain to get back to even?

$100 → $50 has to double to get back to $100.

6. Account $20,000. Most you'd lose: 1%. Buy at $40, wrong below $36. How many shares does the risk-based method give?

1% of $20,000 is $200. Risk per share is $40 − $36 = $4. $200 ÷ $4 = 50 shares, a $2,000 position (10% of the account).

7. You sell a stock at a loss and buy it back two weeks later. Which US tax rule applies?

Buying the same stock back within 30 days of selling at a loss delays that loss for tax purposes.
